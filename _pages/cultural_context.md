LLMs and LLM research have also until recently been English-centered (for good/valid reason), and only recently have efforts begun to improve their capability in other languages. With this, though, is the fact that understanding and appreciating the cultural context around LLM deployment and use cases in different places is (in my view) very nascent. It feels as though in the US, people have largely begun to understand the deployment cases and the possibilities for the technology -- as well as the guardrails and safety/alignment questions that come with those deployments. But, the set of use cases and guardrails in other countries can look totally different. I think it is important to develop robust LLMs in other languages, as well as understand the sort of language-related questions that come with multilingual LLMs, in order to build a better appreciation for the use cases we can find in other settings. Ultimately, this will allow people to build truly transformative technology using these models that can help protect culture and broaden knowledge access.

That is something that motivates the Hindustani language question that I write about here. The Urdu language is historically an Indian one, born in the cities of Delhi and Lucknow and refined in the Deccan centuries ago. It held a major role in British India as a common medium for writing -- whether that be newspapers, poetry, history, revolutionary pamphlets, and more. A lot of Indian history is written in Urdu and its connection to various cultural aspects of India is strong. Yet, the state of the language today in the areas of its historical development is disappointing. Due to a lot of communal politics (too much to go into here, but you can read about the [Hindi-Urdu controversy]() for more), today in India the language is often regarded as belonging to the Muslim community, and since independence has systematically been removed in favor of modern standard Hindi. In Pakistan, meanwhile, Urdu is the official language of the state -- but few of the population speak it natively, and the mother tongues of the country's population (including Punjabi, Sindhi, Balochi, and many others) are also often looked down upon in favor of Urdu. I mention the story of Urdu in Pakistan because of a recently funded YC startup called [Uplift AI](), who are using LLMs to enable text to speech at a scale large enough to broaden the Pakistani population's access to online knowledge -- both in Urdu and in regional languages like Sindhi and Balochi. It's the sort of application of LLMs that really excites me. From my perspective as an Indian-American, whose family is from Delhi, I have become interested in using LLMs where possible to preserve the status of Urdu in India. It's a language with fewer and fewer speakers in the broader Indian community and yet I feel that with more access to the treasure of Indian history and art written in Urdu, people would gain new perspective on the country and the language rather than getting caught up in nationalist, communal narratives.

These are the sort of uses that I hope to be able to build one day, and am working towards with projects such as these and the finetuning projects. With better models and broader access comes a suite of sociocultural questions that have the potential to make massive impacts.
