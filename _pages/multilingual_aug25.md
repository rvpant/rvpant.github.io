A summary of the background, motivation, and future work for this project.


## Investigating the Embedding and Generation Properties of Multilingual LLMs

This **ongoing** project aims to explore how the latest large language models are able to handle multilingual text, with a particular focus on Hindi/Urdu, a pair of very closely related South Asian languages spoken across India and Pakistan. Both languages are under-resourced compared to English and other Western languages, and Urdu in particular lacks robust computational resources. The impetus behind this project is to explore ways in which Urdu can be better supported and how the digital infrastructure for Urdu can be developed further.


### Linguistic Background

Hindi/Urdu, linguistically referred to as [Hindustani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustani_language), is a South Asian language widely spoken across the Indian subcontinent. Hindi and Urdu form its two formal standardized registers, with Urdu the national language of Pakistan and one of India's 22 Scheduled Languages, and Hindi the official language of ten of India's states, primarily in the north. Urdu is written in the Perso-Arabic script, and its vocabulary in the formal setting draws extensively from Persian and Arabic. Hindi is written in the Devanagari script, and its formal register draws heavily from Sanskrit. As a result, speakers of Hindi and Urdu can understand each other completely in colloquial speech, but the formal registers of Hindi and Urdu vary greatly -- being based on the same Indo-Aryan language, both share grammatical and syntactical structure, but their vocabularies in the formal setting are very different. This divergence is the result of geopolitical and cultural divides that took strong hold under British rule in India.

Because of their similarilty, modern natural language processing should be "able" to treat these languages similarly, and you might expect a model to be able to "perform" as well in Hindi as it can in Urdu. In practice, however, there are issues with this assumption, particularly when it comes to Urdu:
- because of the numerical prevalence of Hindi speakers using the Devanagari script, and their representation in the West, there is somewhat better support for Hindi-native computing and digital infrastructure when compared to Urdu.
- the Devanagari script itself is more conducive to Western-centric computing: it is written left-to-right, similarly to English, and its characters retain the same shape regardless of their location in a word, which simplifies things like parsing. Grammatically, its plural structures and rules adhere closely to the base patterns one would expect from an Indo-Aryan langauge. The Perso-Arabic script is written right-to-left, and characters can take many different shapes based on their location in a word. The script is distinct from Arabic, with many more letters and written in a different style, and tends to be used for less prevalent languages in the West (including Farsi, Urdu, and numerous other languages stretching from Iran through Pakistan). This introduces difficulty in implementing natural language processing algorithms when much of our computing infrastucture is designed around familiar English-based language patterns, and even the task of transferring learned patterns in Hindi into Urdu becomes difficult due to the divergence in script.
- Urdu's heavy sampling of Persian and Arabic vocabulary means that its linguistic structure mingles Indo-Aryan patterns with Arabic-derived forms. For example, the Arabic plural form shows up often in Urdu, while Hindi almost exclusively uses the native Indo-Aryan plural form. Arabic adverbs, which incorporate specific diacritics and spellings that are absent in Devanagari but visible in Urdu, are more commonly found in Urdu than in Hindi. Importing vocabulary from Arabic means that relationships form betwen Urdu words that are based on the Arabic tri-root system; the Hindi equivalents of these words may lack these relationships if they are derived from Sanskrit. We discuss these points further below.
- Because of the divergence in the formal registers of Hindi and Urdu, written text corpora for both languages are unlikely to be representative of colloquial speech. This introduces a data bias into any NLP work related to these languages.

In short, while both languages are under-resourced in the digital age, the question of how to leverage their relationship in NLP and other computing applications is not well characterized. My goal with this project is to understand better how we can transfer knowledge about Hindi to the Urdu context, and vice vera; characterize the impact of the difference in script on model performance; and provide a survey/benchmark of modern general purpose and multilingual LLMs in the context of these questions.

### Literature Review

Multilinguality is an active area of research in natural language today. Here, I will maintain a list of references that I have used in defining the research question and methodologies here.

- [Ammar et al, 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.01925) explored "Massively Multilingual Word Embeddings" -- the idea of embedding words from a diverse set of languages in the same embedding space.
- [Pires et al, 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.01502) show that M-BERT is able to generalize across languages AND scripts, but does not learn systematic transformations. THey include an example of successful transfer from Hindi to Urdu using finetuning with POS-tagged Urdu data.
- [Cohere](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.03211) conducted an interesting study examining the impact of quantization on multilingual LLMs in particular. Among other notable findings, they show that languages with non-Latin scripts degrade the fastest during quantization. This has implications for my work here because I am running quantized models; it also raises interesting questions as to the mechanism underlying this behavior.

Today, there are general-purpose LLMs such as Meta's Llama family that are trained on multilingual data, and have access to internal translation tools in order to handle out-of-distribution input languages. We also have purpose-built multilingual language models like Cohere's Aya and Mistral AI's Saba, which are either built specifically with a massive set of languages in the training data (the former) or engineered specifically for different languages and cultural contexts (Arabic and the Middle East in the case of Saba).

There has also been an explosion of work in LLMs tailored to generation in South Asian languages, including Hindi and Urdu. We recently saw Alif and UrduLlama, models that build on top of existing open-source models with continual pretraining to better generate for Urdu-native tasks. On the Hindi side, MBZUAI has led an effort in Hindi-native LLM tasks [cite Nanda-10B]. I have some other writing on the state of multilingual research and evals from my lit review perspective, but here it suffices to say that while the technology is taking large steps forward, work remains to be done on understanding the relationships between these models and their structures. Building off of Anthropic's subliminal learning paper and work like Leqi Liu's linear representation transferability hypothesis, I would like to better understand the structures of these models in order to build a more unified understanding of multilingual models or LLMs natively in other languages.

### Research Questions

Broadly, I want to divide this work into two major research questions.

1. Based on **next-token prediction**: how do existing general-purpose and multilingual LLMs perform on next-token prediction in Urdu, for a function like autocomplete? What techniques help transfer knowledge about the language (perhaps from Hindi) or script (perhaps from Farsi or Arabic) to the Urdu setting to improve performance? Here, we will look to do some exploratory studies on model finetuning, starting with multilingual (Llama 3.2 3B, Cohere Aya 8B) and English-focused LLMs and characterizing changes in their model/output structure following finetuning on an Urdu dataset.
2. Related to **embeddings and activations**: how do Hindi and Urdu vocabulary words look in embedding space? To what extent do those spaces overlap and are these models able to learn the new relationships that arise in Urdu vs Hindi? Here, the goal is to leverage recent research in interpretability (including sparse autoencoders for identifying language-specific features, and work on shared representations and learning) to better understand what these models store pre and post-finetuning in a multilingual sense.

The second question in particular is confusing without a motivating example, which I outline here. I referenced in the linguistic background section that the proliferation of Persian and Arabic vocabulary in Urdu introduces new word relationships that may not exist in Hindi. A major example of this is words that are derived from the Arabic tri-root system. An example: 
- take the Arabic root Q-B-L (qaaf-baa-laam): it relates to the English notions of "facing/fronting" (as in standing in front of), or "accepting". This generates the Urdu _qubool_ (accept), but also _istiqbaal_ (welcome, reception) and _mustaqbal_ (future). In English, these three words seem to be somewhat unrelated, but because they descend from the same Arabic root, there is a latent relationship that you might want a language model to learn. Importantly, the pure Hindi equivalents of these three words {_svikar_, _svaagat_, _bhavishya_} are all derived from Sanskrit, and similarly lack this latent etymological relationship. 

Naturally, questions arise: can a language model be taught to learn these relationships in Urdu when they may not transfer from Hindi models? Do these multilingual models learn the relationships themselves? It is easy to imagine a scenario in which clustering words that come from these shared etymological backgrounds can be useful for computing technology; likewise, if models construct representations for this in their latent space, either via superposition or monosemantic features, this is something that can be optimized to improve performance. Finally, what sort of techniques beyond finetuning can help improve the model performance for such use cases -- or how can we maximise performance of large pretrained multilingual models? Ultimately, the first step of this project is to determine some answers to the above questions, with a future goal of transferring some of these learnings into action that can meaningfully impact the landscape of South Asian language-native computing and digital infrastructure. 

With the availability of "natively multilingual" models that have a wider data distribution or are focused on languages like Hindi and Urdu alongside English, there is also a fascinating secondary set of questions that can be asked "cross-lingually". Namely, how might finetuning on one language (or a focus on prompts/generations in one language) affect model performance on another, related language? For example, will Urdu finetuning affect the nature of model logits in Hindi, or cause the model to reflect new biases when prompted on cultural topics in English? Do pathways used to process cultural or linguistic concepts change pre and post finetuning, in target or peripheral languages?


This leads to the final set of research questions:
    - Within-language: how are model logits altered pre and post finetuning? Do embeddings reflect linguistic traits pre/post?
    Are there identifiable pathways in model latent space linked to linguistic or cultural concepts?
    - Cross-language: does finetuning or natively multilingual pretraining affect model logits, pathways, biases, generation
    in other languages? What pathways might control this, and what relationships between languages are important?
    - Social: does language, or finetuning, modulate biases that are expressed by models? If so, how?
