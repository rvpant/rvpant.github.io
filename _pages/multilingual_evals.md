## TLDR/Thesis
Multilingual LLM evals suffer to some extent from the biases that plague multilingual and low-resource NLP: poor representation in pretraining of frontier models, a lack of high quality evaluation data, low representation of linguistic and cultural concerns specific to most languages/contexts, and evaluation frameworks/metrics that fail to capture all aspects of multilinguality. Many multilingual works fall back on LLMs to generate eval suites and translate existing English-native evals into target low-resource langauge, which is unlikely to capture linguistic nuance. As LLMs become a feature of everyday life, it is imperative to develop methods for evaluating multilingual generation in order to more responsibly and holistically develop models for a diverse set of users and cultural contexts.


## Introduction
Multilingual capabilities in LLMs have been an increasing focus over the past two years in particular -- not just through a more diverse set of languages and scripts provided to pretrained foundation models, but through finetuning and interpretability research that aims to better understand how modern LLMs can be adapted to out-of-distribution languages (and how models treat language). There is a vast amount of research into how and whether language models truly "learn" linguistic structure and language-specific features. As I continue to read more, and with the constant developments in the field, one thing that I find myself increasingly curious about is the evaluation of "higher-level" linguistic capabilities of LLMs and multilingual LLMs -- something along the lines of the question, "what sort of linguistic relationships are these models able to learn, and how do they really represent different languages and structures in their latents?". Of course, a lot of computational linguistics work dives deeply into models' ability to learn formal linguistic relationships as well as "fuzzier" relative concepts or representations. But, with my motivations lying ultimately in the world of South Asian languages and leveraging LLMs to increase accessibility across low-resource languages, I feel there remains a gap in how modern LLM research approaches the evaluation of multilingual model capability. 

I think of this a bit like the [superposition]() idea, but for languages in the LLM latent space: how are related/unrelated languages, etymologies, etc represented in these models? And, given the language imbalances that exist in training data and user data today, can these higher-level linguistic relationships be leveraged effectively to improve performance in lower resource languages? One motivating example is the Hindustani (Hindi-Urdu) language -- functionally one spoken language, but with totally different scripts, and (especially in formal settings) a set of divergent vocabulary and grammatical structures as a result of Urdu's sampling from Persian and Arabic terms (and modern Hindi's emphasis on Sanskrit-dervied forms). I've introduced this topic more fully in the introduction of [another post](https://rvpant.github.io/multilingual/), but it serves to motivate this question too. Hindi is generally much better represented in training data for large models today, and the majority of Perso-Arabic script in models' training data is via Arabic (it's easy to check training data distributions to see this). However, if we better understood how things like linguistic roots and plural forms or script differences are stored in model latents, would it be easier to transfer model knowledge to languages like Urdu with lesser representation? Furthermore, if the development and representation language and culture-specific concepts could be clearly identified, could this help in controlling generation (or better driving diversity in generation)? These are questions that extend beyond the example of Hindi/Urdu into a variety of other lower-resoure langauges. Consequently, I began to ask myself what sort of work exists out there not just in multilingual LLM research, but on developing evaluations and interpretability methods that explore this specific question. As I design a small-scale experiment of my own, I hope to track what existing researchers in the field are working on related to this question.

## Literature

One can broadly divide the work on multilingual models/capabilities into three categories.

The first is large-scale multilingual model pretraining -- where teams with computational resources work on continued pretraining to improve model capability in specific languages. Examples include JAIS and Mistral Saba for Arabic, Llama-Nanda-10B for Hindi/English multilingual capabilities from MBZUAI, and UrduLLaMa1.0 from Pakistan, focused on Urdu-native tasks. At the same time, there are existing multilingual models like Cohere's Aya series that are purposefully trained on a diverse set of data, as well as slightly more closed-source initiatives like Sarvam AI / AI4Bharat who have developed a suite of models for Indian languages including Urdu, with an intense focus on dataset curation and quality. This is foundational work that increases the representation of lower-resource languages and improves access to frontier-level models for a wider set of people, as well as enabling research on how language and culture are represented by different models. However, with a focus on the pretraining phase, these works spend less time on probing finer linguistic or representational questions inherent to LLMs.

The second is multilingual interpretability work, which brings many of the methods in LLM interpretability research over to the multilingual context in order to begin to better understand these models' internals. Some examples that are relevant to questions like the example introduced here:
- "Do LLaMas work in English", showing that the latent space of multilingual models is often English-based, with translation heads at the beginning and end of the reasoning process
- multilingual factual recall work from Ellie Pavlick's group at Brown, in which they trace the language of "thought" for factual recall tasks over model layers for a set of languages, as well as showing that models share the same paths forsyntactic concepts across languages
- Alham Fikri Aji's group at MBZUAI does a lot of fascinating research in the multilingual space, including a recent work on sparse autoencoders (SAEs) and their ability to learn monosemantic, language-linked features in the multilingual setting
- "Steering into new embedding spaces", a paper which reiterates the importance of the search for some sort of shared embedding/representation space in the multilingual model context

A lot of this work is incredibly interesting to me; the interpretability approach will continue to be vital as a means of probing model capabilities. But even in these examples, the sort of language-related questions I am asking may not exist; the evaluation aspect is not the focus of these works, but rather untangling representations and pathways in the models. Much like the pretraining work, this is vitally important and something that should form the foundation of any work looking to answer my questions.

The final area of work in the literature is beginning to examine multilingual evals in general. Some important works here include:
- efforts like UrBLiMP and BATAYAN that are aiming to design better datasets and evaluations for Urdu and Filipino respectively, which begin to highlight the issues with existing translated datasets, tasks, and evals
- Roger Levy's group working on designing syntactic evals, which leverages the idea of "syntactic minimal pairs" to characterize how well models perform at learning language-specific syntactic features; this idea of minimal pairs very much inspires what I propose to do as an evaluation method, which I discuss later on
- the aforementioned research from AI4Bharat includes IndicLLMSuite, a bespoke set of benchmarking tools for evaluating models in Indian languages

This is where things start to come together -- there is a clear need for better evaluation methods and metrics for multilingual LLMs, and with the explosion in multilingual models and studies seeking to improve our understanding of their internal representations, it is important to have a strong way of understanding model performance across languages. Many have begun to do this, and I am curious about extending these sort of questions to finer-grained linguistic tasks by leveraging interpretability methods and human linguistic knowledge to probe and better measure these models' learned relationships.
A related aside is the prevalence of machine-translated English evaluations and tasks for evaluating models in different languages. On the back of works such as the [subliminal learning]() work from Anthropic and [persona vectors]() study from OpenAI, I wonder at the biases that LLM involvement might introduce into evaluation suites and datasets that are used for multilingual evals. For low-resource languages in particular, it becomes crucial that the datasets and evaluation methods used to measure mLLM performance are high quality and as bias-free as possible. Building noisy evals on top of models that are not optimized for these languages is dangerous.



## Cultural Context in Multilingual LLMs

Why care about "cultural context" and low-resource language representation? Language models today are an incredibly powerful and transformative new technology; they will fundamentally change how we do work and interact with the world on a daily basis. It's important that this technology be used **thoughtfully** to bring about positive change, preserve culture and humanism, and serve as a tool for uplifting people and improving their access to knowledge. I have written longer form [here]() about the various ways in which we seem to be moving towards harm as a result of LLM proliferation without more principled perspectives on how this technology can be used for social good as well as productivity gains. In short, if people are not constantly asking questions of the technology and its biases, as they might about a piece of news or a story they hear, we will quikcly move towards danger as critical thinking and human capability is outsourced to these models. When it comes to cultural context and biases (including linguistic biases), it is imperative that LLM technology be developed to serve a wide set of people and with an extreme focus on mitigating the various biases that arise from the training data distribution, finetuning context, and inherent differences in where these models are trained and developed versus deployed. Language and culture drive social change -- much like unreliable news sources or politicians create unrest, so too will LLMs that have unseen or unacknowledged biases. A thorough focus on model evaluation in these diverse contexts is crucial to prevent that and ensure that the benefit of this technology is what dominates the coming years, rather than harm.
