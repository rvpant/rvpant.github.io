In a recent post last fall, I wrote a very disorganized overview of the motivation behind this research project. Through a lot of conversations and reading, I've sincce distilled this down into a more straightforward research question: do LLMs "learn"/express etymology in their latent space, and what can that tell us about their abstraction of language? 

Hindi-Urdu formed an interesting minimal pair for a question like this. The semantic and linguistic structure of the languages being effectively identical, but the etymology and the script being different. I had originally proposed finetuning a model (and had even done a finetuning run with Llama 3.2), and using model-diffing approaches to tease out differences. Instead, though, I switched to an eval-first approach: on top of Llama 3.1 (mostly English/code) and 3.2 (multilingual), there are plenty of other LLama finetunes out there for Urdu and for Hindi (Llama-Nanda-10B), and I decided to build a small example set of minimal pair sentences. Two goals: the first is to see how *EACH* model may attend differently to Hindi vs Urdu sentences, and the second is to compare how models that are more multilingual, or finetuned for one specific language, behave differently on Hindi vs Urdu sentences. The hypothesis would be that there is a notable difference, and that controlling this different expression can inform or predict the models' word choice, sentence construction, and even other properties.

I formed a small eval set by hand, by selecting some minimal word pairs (h1, u1) and (h2, u2). Here h1 and u1 are a Hindi and Urdu word with the same meaning and likewise h2 and u2 are two new words with the same meaning. The interesting relationship is that u1 and u2 share a Arabic root (and thus an etymological link), while h1 and h2 do not. Following the Q-B-L root example from my last post on the topic, h1 and h2 here could be _svagat_ and _svikar_, with u1 and u2 _istqbaal_ and _qubool_. I set up two sentence template task in English as follows: "The Hindi word for {x} is {h1}. The Hindi word for {y} is " and "The Urdu word for {x} is {u1}. The Urdu word for {y} is ". The hypothesis: at inference time, LLMs would attend back to {u1} in generating {u2} as the correct completion to the Urdu template sentence -- but that pattern will not exist in the Hindi template, as {h1} and {h2} do not share the etymological link of {u1} and {u2}! Secondarily, I also hypothesize that finetuning on Urdu (or Arabic/Persian) might deepen this pattern, and finetuning on Hindi might weaken it.

Inspired by the indirect object task and Neel Nanda's/ARENA's accesible introductions to interpretability work, I decided to take a first pass at testing this through visualizingi the attention patterns of the models. I wanted to try to find heads where attention might differ meaningfully at the locations of interest given above -- not just whether the final token attends back to the {u1} token and not {h1}, but even whether intermediate token attention patterns are meaningful. Would {y} be attending back to {x} more heavily in the Urdu case? I also wanted to see at what point the models might begin to put these relationships together and identify the correct completion to the sentences. I took two simple tools from interp work to kick this off: transformerlens (for the first question) and logitlens(for the second).

Results from transformerlens have been interesting, and I am still unsure how to interpret them. I aggregated token-level attention patterns to word level, to adjust for the fact that Hindi words are tokenzied a bit "better" than Urdu, resulting in the Hindi sentence having fewer tokens than Urdu (despite the same word count). After normalizing, I then calculated the difference in word-level attention between the Hindi and Urdu template prompts, and visualized those differences for any layer.head that had an (i,j) pair such that abs(diffs(i,j)) was greater than 0.2 (an arbitrary threshold to start). I'll include some visualizations below -- a challenge for me is rigorously determining to what extent these attention diff patterns are meaningful/causal vs incidental, or due to another factor that I am failing to control for. I hope that the logitlens analysis might help point to the extent to which these diffs really are meaningful -- another approach would be to mimic the IOI study and run head ablations, studying the model output changes as a result. I'll share some below alongside some notes, and as I update this post with logitlens results, I hope that more will become clear.
